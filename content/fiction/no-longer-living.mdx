*By Robert Shiraz*

> **Content note:** Contains sexual content, drug use, police violence, and animal death.

I should say at the start that I'm no longer living in the world. It isn't a complaint. It's just where I am now, and from here things are easier to see. What I want to tell about is the hamster.

### 1

The day I decided to buy a hamster, I was standing in the middle of my apartment with my head against the ceiling. It was a studio. The ceiling was low. I'd been standing there a while.

I took inventory. Three pawnshop guitars. An amp. A bass and a bass amp. A kitchen table. A futon mattress without the frame. A stereo from 1986 with speakers the size of bar fridges. A chair, a bigger chair, a beanbag chair. A bearskin rug with cigarette burns in it. The room was full, and everything in it was dead. Myself included, possibly, though I felt that was a verdict best left to history.

My friends had stopped returning my calls four months before. A month before, I'd stopped calling them. It wasn't anybody's fault. People outgrow each other. It's a natural law, like weather, or the decline of the West. That afternoon it was raining, and I caught myself dialling Dave's number to see if he wanted to play some video games or get a beer. I hung up before it rang. Then I stood there with my head on the ceiling and thought about what a person needs. A person needs something alive in the room.

I put on my good sweatpants and went out.

### 2

The guy at the first pet store didn't like me. I could tell right away.

"Are you sure you're ready for that kind of commitment?" he said.

"It's a hamster."

"I've been doing this eleven years," he said. "People come in, they think it's nothing. Half of them are back in a month with an empty cage."

I considered this. Eleven years of empty cages. It gave the man a certain authority, like a veteran of a small, sad war. But I also knew that rats lived in the dumpster behind my building, and they did fine without anybody's commitment. A hamster was only a rat with better PR.

"Do you know the responsibilities of pet care?" he said.

"Do I look like I can't read a pamphlet?"

"Sir, I don't think we have a hamster that's right for you."

I told him where I thought he'd put all the hamsters, and with what kind of pipe. He picked up the phone. He wasn't calling a supplier.

I went to the other pet store. It turned out to be better anyway.

### 3

The other pet store was farther, out past Commercial. The clerk was an old man in a cardigan with a hearing aid. He nodded at me when I came in and went back to his crossword.

I spent a long time at the glass. I wanted the least ratty one. In the end I picked a black Amazonian Miniature, small and shiny as a wet olive. It looked at me and I looked at it. Something was agreed between us.

When I turned around to ask the clerk a question, he was lying on the floor behind the counter. His pen was still in his hand. I hadn't heard anything. Or I had heard something, a sound like a drain clearing, but I'd thought it was the aquarium filters.

I called 911. They said to stay on the line. I stayed on the line. While I waited, I picked out a cage, a bag of cedar shavings, a plastic castle, an exercise wheel, and a box of gourmet hamster food with sunflower seeds in it. The shavings were on a low shelf behind the counter, so I stepped over him to get them, and again on the way back. By the time the paramedics came, the old man had gone a kind of blue-grey, the colour of the sky over the Second Narrows, and I had everything I needed in two bags. I gave the police a statement. I left cash on the counter. It seemed like the right thing.

On the walk home I hummed something to the hamster through the bars. It was a nothing tune at first. Then I noticed it had a three-part harmony in the refrain, and I practised each part, one after the other, all the way back to Hastings.

### 4

That first night I told the hamster about my life. I told it about the band I'd almost been in and the job at Future Shop and the time I saw Lemmy at the airport. After a while I felt like I was talking too much. So I set the cage on top of the speaker and we listened to Sabbath, loud.

In the morning "War Pigs" was skipping in the CD player, the same half second of guitar over and over, and the hamster hadn't moved. The food was untouched. The water bottle was full. I tapped the cage. I turned off the stereo. I opened the top and poked it with one finger. It was shaking all over, so at least it was alive.

An adjustment period, I decided. I left the lid open and went to make breakfast.

It was a Thursday, so I made my grilled cheese. You fry the bacon first. You pour off most of the fat but not all. You butter the bread on the outside and lay it in the pan, cheese, then bacon, then more cheese, then the second slice. It was February, so I used extra mayo. There are rules to these things, even if you're the one who made them.

After breakfast I played the game where you drive a tank into a shopping mall. I'd been stuck on that level for weeks. The level after that was the White House, where people said a secret weapon was hidden in the Oval Office. I blew up the mall until six hours had gone by and I had a headache and a knot in my side from the way I lie when I play. Then I got up off the floor.

The kitchen was the part of the room with linoleum. Lime green, with copper edging where it met the carpet. I put a tray of Tater Tots in the oven. While they cooked I checked my email. There wasn't any. I downloaded twenty new emoticons and sent them to people on Facebook I didn't know. My favourite was a little skull that chewed its own jaw. The oven timer dinged. I got the hot sauce out and sat down and ate, and thought about what I was going to do with the night.

It wasn't an easy decision. It rarely is, for a man with options.

### 5

I left the apartment at seven. Walking downtown, I watched the buses move up Hastings like whales in shallow water. I pictured stepping in front of one. I saw myself burst open like an orange dropped off a roof. It wasn't a plan. It was just a picture, the way you picture a sandwich.

A girl on the bus caught my eye. She looked like a student. She looked smarter than me. She was reading an enormous book with an old leather binding, and it occurred to me that it might be a book of spells. Then the bus pulled away and took her with it, out of what I could see and into what I couldn't. I can't read on buses. It makes me sick. I don't read much anyway, so it evens out.

The plan was to patch things up with the Thursday group. Every week they met in the basement of the Wizard's Warehouse, on Seventh and Main, and entered the realm of Steve. Steve owned the store and was the Dungeon Master. For years I'd helped defend his kingdom from dragons. Then I'd decided I was too old for it and stopped going. Now I had nothing going on, which is a kind of youth.

My character was Gogor the Elfraper, an ogre. Gogor was level twenty-five, the highest there is. He wore the Gauntlets of Thor, plus twenty-two to strength. He carried a sword forged in dragon fire and a ring that cast sleep. He liked killing things and taking their treasure. He'd been a member of the Ogre Fascist Party, though he'd let his membership lapse. His father had once tried to eat him. He had complicated feelings about elves. Gogor was, in other words, fully realized. Most people in literature don't get half that much.

To smooth my return, I decided to bring pot. The guys liked pot and never had any. I headed for Victory Square, where there was usually someone selling, and cut south onto the residential streets. The sky hung low and grey and heavy. I passed a school. Two kids in hoodies were shooting baskets without much interest, smoking. I stopped at the chain-link fence and watched them. They stopped and watched me back.

"What the fuck do you want?" said the tall one.

"Do you guys have any pot?"

"You a cop?" said the other one.

"Do I look like a cop?"

"You look like a guy who buys weed off high school kids."

"Well," I said. A raindrop hit my nose.

"Forty for an eighth," said the tall one. "Give us the money, we'll be back in twenty minutes."

"I used to pay twenty-five."

"Not on the street you didn't."

"I asked if you had any. I didn't ask to fund your afternoon. You don't have any on you?"

The short one looked at his shoes and kicked a pebble. "A little." He took out a baggie with something green in it.

"Twenty," I said.

"Fine," said the tall one. He took the baggie from his friend and came up to the fence. I held up my twenty. He held up the baggie. We watched each other. We made the exchange slowly, in stages, each of us touching the other thing before letting go of our own. For a second we were joined, the two of us, in a closed loop of money and drugs, and there was something almost holy in it. Then we let go at the same time.

"Cops!" yelled the short one.

They ran around the side of the school. I ran the other way, along the fence and down the street, arms going, legs going. I made it a block and a half before my lungs gave out and I had to sit on the curb. There were no police anywhere. It started to rain for real.

### 6

I got to the Wizard's Warehouse at eight and knocked on the glass. It took Steve a while to come up from the basement. When he saw me, his face changed, like someone had told him a relative was sick. He opened the door partway.

"What do you want?"

"I thought I'd come back to the game. I brought pot." I held up the baggie.

He took it and looked inside and handed it back. "This is oregano."

"No."

"Pretty sure. Anyway, the guys don't want you back. Even with pot."

"Why?"

He laughed. "Why? Your character tried to rape Jim's elf."

"My character's name is Gogor the Elfraper. I was playing in character."

"Jim was pretty upset."

"Jim's character stole the Golden Chalice of Steve. What did he think a fascist ogre was going to do? He's a thief. He got caught."

"We annulled your action and kicked you out. Remember? Also Jim's elf is a man."

"What difference does that make? You've never seen an ogre woman. Next to an ogre woman, a male elf is a ten."

"It's a game," Steve said. "It's supposed to be fun. Nobody wants to play with a guy who does that. You wrecked it."

"It's like acting, Steve. You have to be true to the character."

"Please just leave."

"Steve. Don't be an asshole."

Steve drew himself up to his full five foot nine. "Enough," he said, in the voice he used for dragons. "The Dungeon Master has spoken. Begone." He shut the door and went down the stairs. A minute later he came back up and locked it.

I walked home. It was not the first time an artist had been run out of town by a committee.

### 7

When I got in, something felt wrong with the apartment, but I couldn't say what. I decided to smoke the oregano. I packed it into my old black bong, the one with the silver skull for a bowl, and pulled hard. It burned like campfire smoke, but savoury, like a pizza on fire. I coughed so long I nearly choked. When I was done coughing I checked in with myself. I hadn't been high in a long time, and I'd never smoked oregano. But I felt a little high. Either the bag wasn't all oregano, or oregano had powers nobody had told me about. I smoked the rest.

There was one message on the machine.

"Mr. Spazzing, this is Sergeant Montgomery, Vancouver Police. We've reviewed the security footage from Stepp's Pets from yesterday evening, and we have some further questions. The footage shows you stepping over Mr. Stepp. Twice. We haven't settled on a charge yet. Criminal negligence, possibly. We'll figure it out when you come in. Frankly, sir, I've been doing this a long time, and I've never—"

I turned it off. I wondered for a second what they wanted. Then I looked at the speaker, and the cage on top of it was empty.

I looked everywhere. In the cupboards. Under the chairs, all three. Under the bearskin rug. There was no hamster. It was gone the way the girl on the bus was gone, out of the part of the world I could see. I felt let down, but not surprised. At least I hadn't paid for it. Well, I had left some cash.

I watched TV. I had a hundred and thirty channels and followed five reality shows at once, flipping between them, until they ran together and I couldn't tell the girl who'd betrayed everyone on the island from the girl about to make it big in Nashville. Maybe she was both.

I turned off the TV.

### 8

The Cobalt usually had a black metal show on Thursdays. I decided to go, to take my mind off the hamster. I started out walking, but my legs felt like they didn't have bones in them, so I took the bus. The band was still setting up. I ordered a rye and Coke and leaned on the bar, and that's when I saw Pam. She saw me too. She came over with her small face pinched tight.

"You are such an asshole," she said.

"Hey, Pam. Good to see you." Then it reached me what she'd said. "What's the matter?"

"You never called me back. It's been months."

"I thought you broke up with me."

"I said maybe we should think about taking a break. That's not the same."

"It isn't?"

"It means we talk about it more. Try to work it out. Like grown-ups."

"Is that what grown-ups do?"

"They try."

"I guess I missed that part," I said. "You were talking like it was over. A man has to protect himself."

"I drove you to Emergency when you broke your wrist," she said. "I sat there six hours."

"It was a ride," I said. "I'd have done the same."

"You never did, though."

She leaned in close. "You know what I think this is really about? The anal thing."

"You brought that up. Not me."

"You didn't say anything?"

"Not at first."

"I was drunk. I was curious. It was just conversation." She was going red.

"It wasn't even in my head till you said it."

"And then you were obsessed."

"Now you're being silly."

"Every time, you brought it up." She narrowed her eyes. "Once you tried to just slip it in. Like I wouldn't notice."

"That was an accident. You know I have no sense of direction."

That was true, and so was the rest. I'd been fine before she mentioned it. But once she'd put it out there, I couldn't stop thinking about it. It came to me in my sleep, like a door in a house I'd lived in for years and never noticed. And as soon as she'd said it, she'd closed that door and never let me bring it up again.

"And then I said maybe someday I'd let my husband try it," she said, "and you stopped calling."

"I stopped calling when I thought you'd dumped me. That was way after the husband conversation. Anyway, I've never even done it. How would I know if it's worth getting married for?"

"Fuck you," she said, and walked out.

I shrugged. The band was starting. They were called Bloodtroll, and the singer performed in a butcher's apron and threw pig guts into the crowd. The first song was about summoning trolls to battle. Under the strobe lights the drummer had eight hands, and the guitarists' fingers smeared across the necks. The guts flew. None of them hit me.

Between songs a girl in white makeup and blood-red lipstick held a drink out to me.

"Some weird guy just gave me this," she said. "You want it?"

I looked down into the glass. When I looked up, she was gone.

### 9

By the third band I was drunker than I should have been. I always count. One rye and Coke per band, plus whatever the vampire girl had given me. Rum, maybe. Then I understood why she hadn't wanted it. The room tipped. I went for the door.

Outside, everything smeared. The sky glowed orange with the city under it. Cars went by too fast. People's faces melted as I passed them. My feet were willing, but the ground wasn't.

I walked for what might have been an hour, or ten minutes. I didn't know where I was. I tried to make myself throw up. Two guys came toward me, and I asked them where I was. What came back was noise. They might have been laughing. The shorter one took something out of his pocket and held it up, like he wanted to show me. I leaned in to look.

Something hit my face, wet. I stood there. The smell was familiar. It took me a while to put it together. I tried to say what the fuck and swing at him. They laughed and pushed me down on the sidewalk, and then my face caught fire.

I screamed. I threw up. My eyes were sealed shut. I heard them running off. I crawled until I found a wall and sat against it with my face in my hands. I rubbed my eyes with my sleeves, which made it worse. I called for help. Only cars answered. If my eyes hadn't been streaming already, I would have cried. I tried to get up once and couldn't, so I sat and waited.

It took a long time for my eyes to open. When they did, just slits, it was morning. I got up and walked. A car stopped when I waved. I hoped it was a cab. I got in.

"No, no, no," the driver said. "What happened to you? You can't come in here."

"Please. I can't see. Just take me home."

"You're making my eyes burn. Get out."

"I got pepper-sprayed. Roll the windows down. Please."

"Who sprayed you?"

"Some guy."

"Not a woman?"

"No. Some guy. Here. It's all the money I have."

He rolled every window down and drove with his head out the side like a dog. I kept my eyes shut. He let me out in front of my building. It took me a long time to get the key in the lock. Inside, I went straight for the bathroom. Something squished under my foot, soft but also crunchy. A Tater Tot, I figured. I didn't stop. I took my clothes off and sat in the tub with my face turned up into the cold water, and I fell asleep like that. Later I woke up and dried off and went to bed.

### 10

The buzzer woke me. Everything from the night before came back at once. I put on my robe and buzzed whoever it was in without asking. My eyes still burned. The smell was still in my skin and my hair.

While I waited for them to come up, I heard squeaking from under the sink. Right away, I felt better. I opened the cupboard and got down and looked past the garbage and the spilled scouring powder, into the very back, where the light doesn't reach. Behind an old chip bag, with a banana peel from last spring inside it, I saw something I would not forget for the rest of my life.

Considering how long the rest of my life turned out to be, that isn't saying much.

I pulled my head out of the cupboard, smiling, and hit it on the drainpipe. I went down face-first into the scouring powder and breathed it in. It found everything the pepper spray had already found. Someone was knocking. I reached up for the counter, and my hand closed on the handle of a frying pan. A sound came out of me that I'd never made before. The knocking turned into kicking.

Two police officers came through the door. I turned toward them, mostly blind, with the pan in my hand and that sound still coming out of me. "Drop it," one of them said. The pan slipped. It went past his head. "He's high," the other one said. "Look at his eyes." He drew his Taser without hurrying, like a man reaching for his keys. I tried to explain. Powder came out. He fired, and the darts caught me across the mouth, and my head went back, and something in my throat closed and didn't open again.

The officers put a knife in my hand.

What I saw under the sink was the hamster, nursing a litter. It had been a girl the whole time. There were six babies. I think there had been seven. In the two months it took to clean the apartment and rent it out again, the babies grew up. Then they had babies. Now they live in the walls of the building, in the vents, in every space too small for anyone to look into. They're small and black and shiny, like wet olives. People hear them at night and don't know what they're hearing.

I'm no longer living in the world. But the hamster is.

---

*Robert Shiraz lives in a basement in East Vancouver with a guitar that has been pawned four times and redeemed three. He has outlived two hamsters and one engagement, and considers the hamsters the greater loss.*
